Methods & Sources

Data

A single Esri file geodatabase layer, CP_Hiring_Exercise_Missoula (32,974 parcels, NAD83 State Plane Montana, US feet), with an accompanying field map. Fields used: existing use (LandUseBuildingType_All), dwelling units, assessed land/building/total value, acres, Growth Policy future land use (Land_Use), current zoning (Zoning_Mstr), percent of parcel in floodway (PercentageFlood), and percent in steep slopes > 25% (PercentSlope).

Cleaning decisions

Quirk Treatment
~900 features stored as curve geometries (MultiSurface) Linearized to polygons on read (GDAL forceTo)
PercentageFlood / PercentSlope populated only where a constraint exists NA → 0% (verified: nulls mean “no mapped constraint”)
YearBuilt = 0 sentinel → NA
Invalid rings / self-intersections Repaired with st_make_valid() on the invalid subset
Empty geometries Dropped

Two source-labeling notes: the PercentageFlood field is aliased “Percentage of Floodplain in Parcel” but described as percent in floodway — the write-up says “floodway/floodplain” advisedly, and the constraint screen is identical either way. The Mullan-area designation is spelled Sxtpqyen Area in the data; prose uses the City’s common rendering “Sxwtpqyen.”

Plan-enabled capacity

For each parcel whose Growth Policy designation calls for housing:

capacity = developable acres × assumed du/ac − existing units, floored at zero

where developable acres = acres × (1 − max(flood share, slope share)). Taking the max of the two constraint shares is conservative without double-counting (their overlap is unknown).

Allowable densities come from the adopted Our Missoula 2035 Growth Policy designation ranges, as those ranges are applied in the City’s own annexation and rezoning staff reports (e.g., “Residential Medium Density (3 to 11 dwelling units per acre)… Residential High Density (24 to 43 dwelling units per acre) (Our Missoula 2035)”). Capacity uses the midpoint of each range — a deliberately conservative “expected” density rather than the plan’s ceiling:

Designation Policy range (du/ac) Used
Residential Low 1–2 1.5
Residential Medium 3–11 7
Residential Medium-High 12–23 17.5
Residential High 24–43 33.5
Neighborhood Mixed Use carries medium-high (12–23) 17.5
Community Mixed Use carries high (24–43 in aligned zoning) 33.5
Urban Center not stated in the Policy 43

Urban Center has no du/ac in the Policy; it is pinned at 43 — the highest density the Policy quotes anywhere — which is conservative for the downtown designation. Values are centralized in code/00_setup.R. All other designations (including the Sxwtpqyen Area’s own designation, industrial, parks, open/resource) contribute zero capacity — a conservative choice that likely understates the total.

The opportunity screen

A parcel counts as an opportunity when all of the following hold:

  1. Housing-eligible future land-use designation (table above);
  2. Economically soft — vacant, or improvement-to-land ratio (ILR: assessed building value ÷ assessed land value) < 1.0, i.e., the dirt is worth more than what stands on it;
  3. Not excluded — public/institutional land, parks, open space and recreation, master condo records (which would double-count their child units), and mobile homes (naturally occurring affordable housing — flagged separately as displacement risk, never as opportunity);
  4. Not constrained — no more than 50% of the parcel in floodway or steep slope; capacity on the remainder is already discounted;
  5. Material — capacity gap of at least one unit.

Opportunity parcels are typed vacant vs. underbuilt and sized infill (≤ 5 ac) vs. large site (> 5 ac). The 5-acre line separates parcels a single owner or small builder can develop under by-right rules from sites that realistically need master planning, subdivision, and new internal infrastructure.

Plan vintage

The taxlot layer carries the future land-use designations of the 2035 Growth Policy. In December 2024 the City adopted the successor Our Missoula 2045 Land Use Plan (need identified: ~22,000–27,500 new homes by 2045). Capacity here is therefore measured against the 2035-vintage map and read as a feasibility check on the 2045 target — designation-level densities are broadly continuous between the two plans, but a designation-by- designation re-run on the 2045 map is the natural refresh once its GIS layer is in hand.

Naturally occurring affordable housing (NOAH)

NOAH = parcels with at least one dwelling unit whose total assessed value per unit falls in the bottom quartile of the city’s housing stock — everything under ~$240,300 per unit (6,691 parcels, ~23,800 units). Assessed value proxies price level, not rent, and lags the market; the flag marks relative affordability, not income-qualified affordability. Exposure is measured on the hex grid: a hex’s NOAH units count as exposed when the hex is, or touches, a 95%+ capacity cluster (~2,600 units).

Transit accessibility

Stops and route shapes come from Mountain Line’s published GTFS feed (July 2026; data/external/). A parcel counts as transit-accessible when it intersects a quarter-mile (1,320 ft) Euclidean buffer around any stop — the standard walkshed shorthand, which slightly overstates true network walking distance. 58% of plan-enabled units on opportunity parcels fall inside the walkshed.

Hot-spot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi*)

Plan-enabled units on opportunity parcels are summed to a 1,000-ft hexagonal grid (~20-acre cells; 1,188 hexes contain at least one housing-eligible parcel). Gi* runs on the hex values with queen contiguity, self-inclusive weights; classes are reported at 90/95/99% confidence.

Significance and multiple testing. With 1,188 hexes, fixed z-score thresholds involve many simultaneous tests: at the 95% level, ~30 hexes would clear the bar by chance alone, against 40 observed — so the 95% tier is treated as an outer envelope, not headline evidence. The finding rests on the 99% tier: 29 hexes observed against ~6 expected by chance (~11,400 units). Under a one-sided false-discovery-rate correction (q < 0.05), 25 hexes (~10,800 units) remain significant — the concentration conclusion is unchanged. Gi* here uses the analytic normal approximation (no permutations), read as descriptive spatial inference rather than formal hypothesis testing.

Why hexes rather than parcels? Parcel-level unit gap is extremely right-skewed (median 1, maximum ~3,200), and parcels vary enormously in size, so a parcel-level statistic mostly re-identifies individual mega-parcels and their immediate neighbors. Aggregating first asks the policy-relevant question — which places, at the scale of a block cluster, concentrate capacity — and yields legible corridors instead of scattered outliers. A uniform grid also equalizes the spatial support underlying each observation (parcels differ in area by four orders of magnitude), which contiguity-based weights otherwise distort.

Why Gi* rather than a LISA map? The question this analysis asks is specific: where do high-capacity places sit next to other high-capacity places — LISA’s high-high quadrant. A Gi* hot spot is precisely that: because the statistic sums each hex together with its neighbors, a significant positive z-score can only occur where high values cluster against other high values. So Gi* delivers the high-high answer directly, with a single confidence-graded class that maps cleanly.

LISA (local Moran’s I) reports the same high-high clusters but adds three more classes — low-low, and the high-low / low-high spatial outliers. Those are valuable diagnostics (an isolated mega-parcel in a low-capacity neighborhood is a high-low outlier), but for a policy audience they quadruple the legend to answer a question nobody asked here. The one thing LISA would add — cold spots (low-low) — turns out empty: no low-capacity cluster reaches significance in this data, which is why the map legend shows only capacity clusters. Running LISA as a robustness check on the hex grid is a natural QC step for the regional expansion.

Cluster naming

The three labeled clusters on the maps are contiguity components of the 95%+ hexes; their names were verified by reverse-geocoding each component’s centroid (North Reserve corridor; Halle Drive area = Linda Vista / Miller Creek; Hillview Way / Moose Can Gully = South Hills).

Reproducibility

Rscript code/run_all.R   # raw .gdb -> derived data -> figures -> map

Numbered scripts with all assumptions centralized in code/00_setup.R; the exact package environment is recorded in output/session_info.txt. The website is rendered with Quarto from the committed figure and map outputs, so rendering never re-runs the analysis.

Limitations

  • Plan-implied, not predicted. Capacity measures what the adopted future land-use map makes room for — not ownership, feasibility, or infrastructure timing.
  • Densities are range midpoints, not the plan’s ceiling — using the top of each designation range would roughly double the headline capacity. Urban Center’s value is an assumption (the Policy states no du/ac for it).
  • Assessed values proxy economic softness; they lag the market and compress at the top.
  • The Sxwtpqyen Area (766 parcels) is conservatively excluded from capacity; the Mullan-area master plan is largely residential, so totals are understated.
  • Zoning is not analyzed here — the story is deliberately framed against the Growth Policy. A zoning-vs-plan entitlement-gap analysis is the natural next phase.
  • ACS estimates (tenure, rent burden) carry margins of error; figures cited are point estimates for Missoula city.
  • The walkshed is Euclidean, not network-routed, and GTFS reflects current service.
